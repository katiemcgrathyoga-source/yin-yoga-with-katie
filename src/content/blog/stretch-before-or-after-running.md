It's the question I get asked more than almost any other, usually with a slightly guilty tone: *am I meant to be stretching before I run, or after?*

The short answer is after, or on a rest day. But the reason why is more useful than the rule, because once you see what stretching is actually for, the timing question mostly answers itself.

## Stretching isn't a warm-up

This is the part that surprises people. A warm-up is meant to raise your body temperature, get blood into the muscles you're about to use, and rehearse the movement you're about to do. Standing still holding a hamstring stretch does none of those things.

So if you stretch for five minutes by the front door and then set off, you haven't warmed up. You've stretched, while cold, and then started running cold.

The thing that warms you up for running is running — gently. A few minutes of walking, then an easy jog, then letting the first mile be slower than you'd like. That's it. If you want something more deliberate, some leg swings and a few slow squats will serve you better than a held stretch.

## What stretching is actually for

It's for range of motion. Holding a shape, regularly, over weeks, gradually gives you more room in a joint — and running quietly takes that room away, a little at a time, especially at the front of the hip.

That's a real and worthwhile thing. It's just a slow, cumulative thing, not something that happens in the ten minutes before a race. You're not preparing your legs for today's run. You're keeping them from getting shorter over the season.

It's also worth being clear about what it isn't. Stretching isn't a fix for a niggle, and it won't undo a week of running more than your legs were ready for — that's a training question rather than a flexibility one. If you'd like the same honesty about soreness, I wrote about [what actually helps sore legs](/blog/sore-legs-after-running/), and the answer isn't stretching there either.

So do it for what it actually gives you: room in your hips, and a quiet ten minutes. That's plenty to be getting on with.

## Why after a run is the sweet spot

Two reasons, and the second is the bigger one.

First, warm tissue is more willing. After a run you're heated through, everything moves more easily, and you can settle into a shape without fighting it. It's simply a nicer place to do this work from.

Second — and this is the part most runners miss — the run isn't what leaves you tight. It's the run *plus* everything after it. You finish, you shower, and then you sit down for the rest of the day. The hip flexors that a run already asked to stay a bit short are now folded up in a chair for eight hours. That's the combination your hips are complaining about, and the fifteen minutes after your run is the one window where you can interrupt it.

So the real argument for stretching after isn't physiological. It's that it's the only time it reliably happens.

## And rest days, which nobody mentions

If I could pick one, I'd pick a rest day. You're not tired. You have the patience for a three-minute hold rather than a thirty-second one. And there's nothing afterwards that needs your legs to feel powerful, so you can go as deep and as slow as you like.

Warm up a little first — a few minutes of moving about the house is genuinely enough — and then take your time. This is where the long, quiet Yin holds belong, and where most of the actual range comes back. [Dragon](/poses/dragon) for the front of the hip and [Sleeping Swan](/poses/sleeping-swan) for the glute behind it are most of what a runner needs, and three minutes in each is plenty.

## The simple version

If you'd like one rule to carry around:

**Move to warm up. Stretch when you're warm.**

Before a run, walk and jog. After a run, or on a rest day, find a few shapes and stay in them — three minutes at about 60–80% of what you could do, nothing forced. That's the whole thing.

Ten quiet minutes after most runs will do more for you than an hour once a fortnight. It always does.

## A note on pain

Everything here is about ordinary tightness, the kind that builds and eases. Sharp pain, a catch on a particular movement, or something that's been there for weeks belongs with a physio rather than a stretch. Yin can sit alongside good treatment. It isn't a substitute for it.

*A gentle note: this is general education, not medical advice. Yin helps with everyday tightness. It doesn't treat or prevent injury, and sharp or lasting pain belongs with a physio or doctor.*
