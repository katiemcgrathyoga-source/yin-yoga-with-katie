You know the scene. You finish a good run — legs heavy, hips tight, that familiar pull down the back of your thigh. You mean to stretch. You check your watch instead, and head for the shower.

I get it. Nobody ever made the stretching part *simple*, so it quietly slides — run after run — and the tightness stacks up. Hips one month, calves the next. Then one morning your stride feels short and braced, and you're wondering where your easy miles went.

Here's the good news: undoing it doesn't take much. Fifteen unhurried minutes, in the right shapes, often enough. That's genuinely most of it. Here's exactly what to do.

## Why after a run, not before

There's a lot of confusing advice about runners and stretching, so let me keep it plain.

Before a run, your body wants to be *woken up*, not lengthened — a few minutes of easy movement does that. Long, deep holds right before you run can actually leave you feeling a little loose and less powerful. So we save them for later.

After a run is when the long, still holds earn their keep. You're warm, the work is done, and this is the moment to give back the range running quietly takes away — and to let your body shift down into its rest state, which is the recovery half of training most runners skip.

## Why fifteen minutes is enough

This is Yin yoga, and all it really means is this: you settle into a shape and stay for a few quiet minutes — nothing forced, only ever to about **60–80% of your capacity** — and let gravity and your breath do the slow work a rushed stretch can't reach.

A hurried thirty-second stretch barely touches the deeper tissue around your hips and joints. That tissue responds to *time*, not effort. Which is oddly freeing: you don't have to work hard here. You mostly have to stay.

You don't need to become flexible. You just need to stop getting tighter.

## The routine

Four shapes, and that's genuinely all. Take a slow half-minute between them — that pause is part of the practice, not a gap in it. If you only have five minutes, do the first two; they're where runners hold the most.

**1. [Dragon](/poses/dragon) — for the hip flexors.** A low lunge, back knee down. Running plus sitting all day shortens the front of the hip more than anywhere else, so we open it first. Let the hips sink toward the floor and breathe. *Two minutes each side.*

**2. [Sleeping Swan](/poses/sleeping-swan) — for the glutes and outer hip.** Front shin angled across the mat, back leg long, then fold forward over the front leg. This is the one runners tend to *feel* most. Ease in slowly. *Two minutes each side.*

**3. [Caterpillar](/poses/caterpillar) — for the hamstrings and the whole back line.** Both legs long, and a slow fold over them with the back rounded and the head heavy. Your hamstrings do the hardest braking work in every stride, so give them their length back — softly, no yanking. *Two minutes.*

**4. [Reclined Twist](/poses/reclined-twist) — for the lower back, and to settle.** On your back, draw the knees across your body and let them drop. Every run is impact your spine absorbs; this gently undoes the day's compression, and it's a lovely place to finish. *One minute each side.*

That's the whole thing. Hips, glutes, hamstrings, spine — the chain running tightens, in about fifteen minutes.

## Making it stick

The routine that helps is the one you actually do. So keep the bar low: roll out your mat after your next run, do what you remember, and let it be imperfect. Little and often beats long and rare every single time — showing up is what quietly moves the needle.

*A gentle note: this is general education, not medical advice. Yin helps with everyday tightness — it doesn't treat or prevent injury, and sharp or lasting pain belongs with a physio or doctor.*
