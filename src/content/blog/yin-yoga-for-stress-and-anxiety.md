Stress doesn't stay in your thoughts — it settles in the body, in a tight jaw, raised shoulders, a chest that never quite finishes exhaling. You can't always think your way out of that, but you can, gently, stretch and breathe your way out of it. A slow Yin practice won't solve whatever's causing the stress, but it gives your nervous system a real chance to come down off high alert, even if only for twenty-five minutes.

## How does Yin Yoga calm stress and anxiety?

Stress keeps the body a little braced, even when there's nothing left to brace against — shoulders creeping up, breath staying shallow and high in the chest, long after the moment that caused it has passed. Yin works against that directly. The **long, still holds** — two minutes or more in each shape — give the body time to notice there's nowhere to run and nothing to fight, and slowly let the bracing go. The **slow, supported breathing** that comes with staying still is widely associated with a shift toward the body's rest state, which is the physical opposite of the wound-up, alert feeling anxiety brings.

None of this is about pushing through anything. Every shape is well supported — by a block, a cushion, or simply the floor — so there's nothing to hold yourself in and nowhere to perform. You're not doing the calming. You're just getting out of its way.

## What if you can't sit still?

If your mind is racing, being told to "just relax" rarely helps — it can even make things worse. This practice doesn't ask you to empty your mind or force yourself calm. Almost every shape below is lying down or propped, so there's very little muscular effort holding you there and nowhere you need to balance. And if your mind wanders off mid-hold — it will, and that's fine — you don't need to chase it back. Just notice, and gently rest your attention on your next breath. That noticing and returning, over and over, is the whole practice. There's nothing to get right.

## The best Yin Yoga poses for stress and anxiety

A short sequence built from just a handful of grounding, well-supported shapes can do a surprising amount. Each links to its own page with cues, hold times and things to watch for:

- **[Child's Pose](/poses/childs-pose)** — a small, folded-in shape to arrive in, forehead resting down.
- **[Caterpillar](/poses/caterpillar)** — a long, quiet forward fold that lets the whole back body soften.
- **[Supported Bridge](/poses/supported-bridge)** — hips lifted onto a block, so the front of the body can open without any effort to hold yourself there.
- **[Reclined Twist](/poses/reclined-twist)** — a gentle wring-out for the spine, lying on your back.
- **[Legs Up the Wall](/poses/legs-up-the-wall)** — a gentle inversion that's often the single most settling shape in the whole practice.
- **[Corpse Pose](/poses/corpse)** — the final rest, where whatever's left of the day is allowed to go.

## How often should you practice for stress relief?

Any time you feel it building is a good time — a midday reset when the day's got away from you, or a slow half hour in the evening to put it down before dinner. There's no minimum that counts: even the six or seven minutes it takes to hold Child's Pose and Legs Up the Wall back to back is a complete practice. What tends to help most isn't one long session but coming back to it regularly, so your body starts to recognise the shape of "coming down" and finds it a little faster each time.

*This is a gentle movement practice, not medical advice or a treatment for anxiety. If you're managing a diagnosed anxiety or panic disorder, please treat this as a complement to the support you already have, not a replacement for it — and if a pose ever raises your distress rather than settling it, come out and rest in whatever position feels safest. If you're pregnant or managing a back, hip or heart concern, take the gentler option in each shape and check with your doctor if you're unsure, especially before inversions like Legs Up the Wall.*
